# 1. Introduction

Large language and multimodal foundation models have reached high scores on many medical knowledge benchmarks, but benchmark accuracy is not equivalent to a reliable decision interface. Reviews of health-care LLM evaluation have found that accuracy dominates published evaluation while calibration, uncertainty, deployment behavior and other dimensions receive much less attention [@bedi2025evaluation]. Medical probability studies further show that how confidence is elicited matters: verbalized probabilities, token probabilities and sampling-based proxies can disagree substantially [@gu2024probabilistic; @savage2025uncertainty].

This distinction matters when a model output is consumed by software rather than read as prose. A downstream system may route a case, call a tool, request evidence, escalate to a clinician, or trigger a thresholded workflow using a probability vector. In that setting, two outputs with the same top-1 answer can lead to different actions if their probabilities differ. Conversely, a model that remains confident after necessary evidence is removed can be operationally unsafe even if its output schema is valid.

Recent typed-decision systems make this problem especially visible. They provide structured probability distributions over explicit options and can reduce decoding cost, but schema validity does not guarantee semantic validity. Work on Jev-like systems has reported option-name/rubric failures, rejection bottlenecks and calibration limitations, while Dyad and related architectures show that candidate semantics can be represented explicitly. These findings weaken any claim that a typed head itself is a new decision paradigm.
A second literature exposes a related but broader failure. Multiple-choice LLMs are sensitive to option order and label tokens [@pezeshkpour2024order; @zheng2024pride]. Subsequent work proposes permutation-aware training and permutation-equivariant architectures [@zheng2026pagrpo; @jiao2026ember; @egressy2025setllm]. Metamorphic-testing research likewise evaluates whether LLM behavior remains stable under semantics-preserving transformations [@decurto2025metamorphic; @zhou2026lgmt]. Therefore neither order robustness nor semantic invariance alone is sufficient novelty.

We argue that medical decision systems require a sharper contract. A transformation that changes only presentation should not arbitrarily move probability mass between candidate meanings. A transformation that changes clinically relevant evidence should not be treated as another invariance test: the model should change its distribution when the decision semantics change. Reliability requires both properties simultaneously.

This paper formalizes that distinction as **contract equivariance** and **semantic responsiveness**. We study distribution-level behavior rather than only top-1 consistency, because downstream decision policies act on probabilities and risk thresholds. We then propose CRDI, a prospective interface that separates candidate meaning from candidate identifier and uses distinct objectives for meaning-preserving and evidence-changing transformations.

Our intended contributions are conditional on evidence: (1) a formal decision-contract framework; (2) metrics that test distribution-level equivariance and evidence responsiveness; (3) a matched evaluation separating readout, supervision and representation effects; and (4) a reproducible medical study with independent-backbone replication and explicit capability-retention gates. If any contribution fails its prespecified falsification criterion, the corresponding claim will be removed rather than rescued post hoc.
